# Analytical Design And Tufte Principles

## Summary
The reviewed note treats analytical design as more than chart minimalism. The strongest ideas are comparison, causality, multivariate structure, integrated evidence, and layered displays that reward both quick scanning and close reading. Sparklines, micro/macro composition, and explicit evidence documentation all appear as practical tools for making dense information both legible and trustworthy. [src-2026-05-23-001]

## Key Ideas
- Comparison is treated as the fundamental analytical act, so every serious display should answer “compared to what?” rather than merely showing isolated values. [src-2026-05-23-001]
- Good analytical presentations should not stop at pattern description; they should try to expose mechanism, causality, or explanatory structure when the evidence supports it. [src-2026-05-23-001]
- The note strongly favors multivariate displays and integrated presentation of words, numbers, diagrams, and images, arguing that over-reduction hides the real structure of problems. [src-2026-05-23-001]
- Sparklines are positioned as a signature compact device for inline trend communication, especially in tables, prose, and dashboards where full charts would be too heavy. [src-2026-05-23-001]
- Layering and separation matter because dense displays can remain readable when primary data is visually emphasized and secondary scaffolding recedes. [src-2026-05-23-001]
- Micro/macro design is a useful test: a strong display should reveal a clear overall pattern at a glance and also reward close inspection with meaningful detail. [src-2026-05-23-001]
- The note’s extended Tufte-style critique checklist is practical because it adds explicit questions about causality, documentation, layering, and multiscale readability. [src-2026-05-23-001]
